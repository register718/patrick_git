When amoebae of Dictyostelium
discoideum, suspended in buffer, were treated with 100 nM
pulses of CAMP, the extracellular CAMP phosphodiesterase (ePD) activity increased dramatically
and the synthesis of the phosphodiesterase inhibitor (PDI) was repressed. In addition, the time of
appearance on the cell surface of contact sites A, membrane-bound CAMP phosphodiesterase, and
CAMP binding sites was accelerated by 3-4 hr and the concentration
of intracellular
CAMP
increased -20-fold. When the concentration of the CAMP pulse was reduced to 1 n&f, the effect of
the pulses on membrane differentiation
and on the CAMP pool was virtually the same, while the
effect on the ePD-PDI system was reduced. When CAMP was added to the suspension continuously, the nucleotide had no effect on membrane differentiation
and failed to stimulate the
intracellular
CAMP pool, however, the ePD-PDI
system was regulated normally. When the
developmental mutant, HC112, was treated with CAMP pulses, membrane differentiation
and the
level of the CAMP pool were unaffected, while the ePD-PDI system responded to the exogenous
CAMP. In another mutant, HC53, membrane differentiation
was stimulated by CAMP pulses and
this response was accompanied by a sharp increase in the concentration of the CAMP pool. These
results suggest that the ePD-PDI system and membrane differentiation
are regulated independently by exogenous CAMP and that regulation of the ePD-PDI system does not require activation
of the adenylyl cyclase.
INTRODUCTION

Upon starvation, amoebae of the cellular
slime mold Dictyostelium discoideum enter
the developmental phase of their life cycle.
After a preaggregation period of 6-8 hr at
22°C the cells stream into multicellular
aggregates, proceed through various morphogenetic stages, and eventually form a
fruiting body structure composed of highly
differentiated
stalk cells and spores (see
Loomis, 1975). Both cell aggregation and
cell differentiation
have been shown to be
regulated, at least in part, by the nucleotide
3’-5’-cyclic
adenosine
monophosphate
(CAMP)’ (Konijn et al., 1968; Bonner, 1970;

During the preaggregation and early aggregation phases, a number of extracellular
and cellular activities change dramatically.
For example, shortly after starvation, the
extracellular

(ePD) activity decreases, and this drop in
activity can be correlated with the secretion
from the cells of a specific protein inhibitor

These changes are followed closely by the
appearance on the cell surface of several
new activities, including contact sites A
(Beug et al., 1973), CAMP binding sites

Newell, 1975), and a membrane-bound

that there is an appreciable increase in adenylyl cyclase activity during this period
(Klein, 1976; Roos et al., 1977b).

When amoebae, suspended in buffer, are
treated with exogenous CAMP, in the form
of either a single pulse or a series of pulses,
the ePD activity of the suspension increases
and PDI synthesis is inhibited (Klein, 1975;
Tsang and Coukell, 1977; Klein and Darmon, 1977). (Since these two extracellular
activities are regulated in a reciprocal fashion by exogenous CAMP, they will be referred to in this paper as the ePD-PDI
system.) Under the same conditions, artificial periodic pulses of CAMP (but not
CAMP added continuously)
have been
shown to accelerate the appearance on the
cell surface of contact sites A, CAMP binding sites, and mPD (Gerisch et al., 1975a)
and to activate the adenylyl cyclase (Klein
and Darmon, 1977; Roos et al., 1977b).
Based on the latter observation, Klein and
Darmon (1977) suggested that the exogenous CAMP might regulate cell differentiation by stimulating adenylyl cyclase activity, thereby elevating the level of intracellular CAMP and enhancing endogenous signal emission.
To determine if the regulation of the
ePD-PDI system and membrane differentiation by exogenous CAMP is mediated by
a common mechanism involving the adenylyl cyclase, we have measured the different
extracellular and cellular activities and the
intracellular
CAMP pools of cells treated
with CAMP pulses and a continual flux of
CAMP. We have also examined these parameters in two aggregation-deficient
mutants which respond differently to exogenous CAMP. The results suggest that exogenous CAMP regulates the production of
the extracellular
and cellular activities by
different mechanisms and that regulation
of the ePD-PDI system by CAMP does not
require the activation of the adenylyl cy&se.

that, in D. discoideum, extracellular CAMP
regulates the ePD-PDI system and the formation of certain membrane components
by two functionally
distinct mechanisms.
As reported previously (Gerisch et al.,
1975a), we have observed that the time of
appearance of membrane components such

CAMP (Figs. 1B and 3B). Furthermore, the

4), presumably by activating the plasma

the magnitude of this increase was also
relatively constant over a l-100 nkf range

Do measurements of the intracellular
CAMP pools of amoebae treated with exogenous CAMP reasonably reflect the in
uivo activity of the adenylyl cyclase? In
shaken suspensions, the basal adenylyl cyclase activity of amoebae of strain AX2 has
been reported to increase as much as 45
fold after approximately
3-4 hr of starvation (Klein, 1976; Klein and Darmon, 1977;
Roos et al., 1977b). Superimposed upon this
increase in basal activity are rhythmic oscillations in adenylyl cyclase activity which
also begin about this time. These oscihations occur with a periodicity of 5-8 min
and have an amplitude 3-10 times that of
the basal activity (Roos et aE., 1977a; Klein
et al., 1977). Together, these changes in
adenylyl cyclase activity are probably responsible for the sharp increase in the level
of the CAMP pool observed after 3-4 hr in
amoebae of strain AX2 grown axenically
(Fig. 4) and after 5-6 hr in amoebae of
strains HC91 and HC6 grown on bacteria
(Figs. 6 and 8). The subsequent drop in the
concentration of the CAMP pool after 5-6
hr is probably due to enhanced secretion of
the nucleotide by the cells, since extracellular CAMP levels are maximal at this time
(Klein and Brachet, 1975). When amoebae,
under the same conditions, are treated with
artificial pulses of CAMP, both the basal
activity and the periodic oscillations in adenylyl cyclase activity are stimulated after
3-4 hr of starvation (Roos et al., 1977b;
Gerisch and Hess, 1974; Roos et al., 1977a).
Presumably, this activation of the adenylyl
cyclase by CAMP pulses is responsible for
the dramatic increase in the CAMP pools of
the CAMP-pulsed cells observed at this
time (Fig. 4; Table 2). Again, the subsequent drop in intracellular
CAMP is probably due to enhanced secretion. When
CAMP was added to the cell suspensions in
a continuous flow, the CAMP pools of the
cells usually increased slowly two- to threefold and then remained relatively constant

for 6-8 hr (see Fig. 4). Since it has been
reported that the basal adenylyl cyclase
activity is not activated by the continual

Alternatively,
the low level of intracellular
CAMP detected in cells treated with a continual flux of CAMP might be an artifact
due to the elevated cellular PD activity
present under these conditions (Yeh and
Coukell, unpublished). However, this interpretation seems unlikely since cellular PD
levels are also elevated by treating the cells
with CAMP pulses (Klein and Darmon,
1975; Lo and Coukell, unpublished),
and
under these conditions the concentration of
the CAMP pool is very high (Fig. 4). In
general, then, the changes observed in the
levels of the CAMP pools of cells under
different conditions do appear to reflect the
changes occurring in adenylyl cyclase activity under the same conditions.
These results suggest that during the first
few hours of starvation, the amoebae might
form two distinct types of CAMP receptors:
(1) a receptor which detects a change in the
concentration of extracellular CAMP with
time, and responds by inducing a number
of changes in the plasma membrane (and
possibly other events), probably by activating the adenylyl cyclase; and (2) a receptor
which simply detects an increase in the
level of extracellular
CAMP and, without
activating the adenylyl cyclase, increases
the production of ePD and inhibits PDI
synthesis. However, the results of experiments described in this study do not exclude the possibility that a single CAMP
receptor could be involved in regulating
both processes. During this study, we consistently observed a distinct peak of CAMP
